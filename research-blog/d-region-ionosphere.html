The D-region Ionosphere

The lowest region of the ionosphere, where solar radiation, atmospheric chemistry and radio-wave propagation come together.

The Earth's ionosphere is commonly divided into three principal regions: the D-region, E-region and F-region. Another region, the C-region, exists below the D-region. However, its ionization level is so low that it has little influence on radio signals or communications and is therefore often disregarded as an ionospheric layer.

Each region of the ionosphere has its own characteristic properties and influences radio-wave propagation in different ways. Their formation and maintenance are also governed by different physical and chemical processes. Understanding these regions individually is therefore important for understanding the behaviour of the ionosphere as a whole.

D-region Ionosphere

The D-region is the lowest part of the Earth's ionosphere, extending approximately from 60 km to 80 km above the Earth's surface. Unlike the higher E- and F-regions, the D-region is characterised by complex ion chemistry and plays an important role in absorbing and modulating Very Low Frequency (VLF) and Low Frequency (LF) radio waves.

The region is strongly influenced by solar radiation, particularly extreme ultraviolet (EUV) and soft X-ray photons. These sources initiate a complex combination of ionization, attachment and recombination processes. Although the D-region has a much lower electron density than the upper ionosphere, its dynamical response to solar activity and its distinctive ion chemistry make it an important region for space-weather and atmospheric studies.

Source of Ionization

Ionization in the D-region is primarily produced by solar Lyman-alpha (Lα) radiation, extreme ultraviolet radiation and soft X-rays. During daytime, solar photons interact with neutral atmospheric constituents such as molecular nitrogen (N2), molecular oxygen (O2) and nitric oxide (NO), producing the primary ionization.

Nitric oxide is one of the principal constituents in the upper part of the D-region and has an ionization potential of approximately 9.25 eV. Consequently, Lyman-alpha radiation at a wavelength of approximately 121.6 nm is particularly important for ionization in the upper D-region.

At lower altitudes, approximately 60–70 km, solar radiation with wavelengths between about 110 and 121.6 nm can penetrate sufficiently to contribute to ionization. Soft X-rays, with wavelengths of roughly 1–10 nm, are particularly effective in ionizing N2 and O2 at these lower altitudes.

Other sources can also contribute to D-region ionization. These include cosmic rays and energetic particle precipitation, particularly during geomagnetic disturbances and solar proton events. Such processes can become especially important during nighttime or at high latitudes.

Chemistry of the D-region

The ion chemistry of the D-region is distinct from that of the higher ionospheric regions because of its relatively low electron density and high neutral atmospheric density.

Positive ions found in the region include N2+ and O2+, which can be produced through photoionization and charge-exchange processes. NO+ is another important and relatively stable ion produced through photoionization and subsequent chemical reactions.

Cluster ions such as H3O+(H2O)n can become important below approximately 75 km, particularly under humid conditions.

One of the distinctive characteristics of the D-region is the presence of negative ions. These include O−, O2− and NO2−, which can form through electron attachment to electronegative molecules. Cluster ions such as CO3−(H2O)n and NO3−(H2O)n may also occur under suitable conditions.

These negative ions become particularly important when the abundance of free electrons is low, such as during nighttime or periods of low solar activity, and influence the electron-density profile of the D-region.

Recombination Processes

The D-region is governed by relatively rapid recombination processes. Two important pathways are dissociative recombination and ion–ion recombination.

Dissociative Recombination

NO+ + e− → N + O

Ion–Ion Recombination

NO+ + O2− → NO + O2

These reactions can significantly reduce electron density, particularly during nighttime, and contribute to the strong variability of the D-region.

In modelling studies, an effective recombination coefficient, αeff, is often used to represent the combined influence of these processes. Its value depends on altitude, temperature and ion composition.

The effective recombination coefficient can be expressed as a combination of the dissociative recombination coefficient αd and the ion–ion recombination coefficient αi:

αeff = αd(1 − λ) + αiλ

Here, λ represents the relative contribution of negative ions to the ionospheric charge balance. When λ = 0, the expression reduces to purely dissociative recombination. At λ = 1, ion–ion recombination dominates. Intermediate values represent a combination of the two processes.

Diurnal and Seasonal Variability

Electron density in the D-region varies strongly with time of day. During daylight hours, solar radiation continuously produces ionization. After sunset, ion production decreases rapidly while recombination and attachment processes continue to remove free electrons.

Consequently, the D-region electron density can decrease dramatically after sunset and the region becomes much less ionized during nighttime.

Seasonal variations in solar radiation and atmospheric composition also influence the electron density and ion chemistry. As a whole, the D-region exhibits a strong dependence on the solar zenith angle, which describes the position of the Sun relative to the local vertical direction.

Importance in Radio-Wave Propagation

One of the most important reasons for studying the D-region is its influence on VLF and LF radio-wave propagation. These signals can travel over very large distances through the waveguide formed between the Earth's surface and the lower ionosphere.

Changes in D-region electron density modify the propagation conditions of these radio waves. During solar flares, enhanced X-ray irradiance can increase the electron density of the D-region, producing measurable changes in VLF signal amplitude and phase.

These variations form the basis of VLF remote sensing, in which measurements of radio signals are used to investigate changes in the lower ionosphere.

Why is this important?

The D-region is difficult to observe directly because of its low electron density and its location at the interface between the neutral atmosphere and the ionized upper atmosphere. VLF radio signals therefore provide a particularly useful indirect means of studying how this region responds to solar activity.

In Summary

The D-region may be the lowest and least ionized part of the ionosphere, but it is far from being unimportant. Its complex chemistry, strong response to solar radiation and influence on VLF and LF propagation make it an important component of the Sun–Earth system.

Understanding how its electron density changes with solar radiation, altitude, time of day and season is therefore essential for understanding both ionospheric physics and the behaviour of sub-ionospheric radio signals.
