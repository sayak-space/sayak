Research

Solar–Terrestrial Physics & Ionospheric Research

My research focuses on understanding the response of the lower ionosphere to solar radiation and developing numerical and radio-remote-sensing approaches to investigate this response.

Research Overview

My primary research interest lies in the D-region ionosphere, the lowest region of Earth's ionosphere, approximately 60–80 km above the Earth's surface.

Unlike the higher E- and F-regions, the D-region is strongly influenced by complex ion-neutral chemistry, frequent collisions and rapid recombination processes. It also responds sensitively to solar radiation, particularly solar X-rays, extreme ultraviolet (EUV) radiation and Lyman-α.

My research has focused on developing an electron continuity-based numerical framework to investigate how the D-region electron density responds to changing solar ionization.

The central theme of my research is to understand how solar variability is translated into measurable changes in the lower ionosphere and, ultimately, into perturbations of sub-ionospheric VLF radio signals.

The D-Region Ionosphere

The D-region is the lowest part of the ionosphere and occupies an altitude range of roughly 60–80 km. It is highly dynamic and is strongly controlled by solar radiation.

During daytime, solar radiation produces ionization in the region, while after sunset the electron density decreases rapidly because of recombination and attachment processes. Consequently, the D-region nearly disappears during nighttime.

Solar X-rays, EUV and Lyman-α radiation play important roles in producing D-region ionization. Because of this strong solar dependence, the D-region provides an excellent natural laboratory for investigating solar–terrestrial coupling.

My research has particularly concentrated on how the electron density of this region responds to sudden changes in solar radiation.

Electron Continuity-Based Modelling

A major component of my research has been the development of an electron continuity-based numerical model for the D-region ionosphere.

The model describes the evolution of electron density through the balance between ionization and recombination. The ionization rate is determined from solar radiation and depends on parameters including solar zenith angle, geographical location, day of year and time of day.

The model can generate altitude-resolved profiles of ionization, recombination and electron density. Initially, the model was developed primarily for the response to solar X-ray radiation during solar flares.

It was subsequently extended to incorporate EUV and Lyman-α contributions, allowing the model to investigate D-region behaviour during periods of comparatively low solar X-ray activity as well.

Ionospheric Response Time Delay

One of the important quantities investigated in my research is the ionospheric response time delay, Δt — the time difference between a change in solar ionizing radiation and the corresponding response of the ionospheric electron density.

The ionosphere does not respond instantaneously to changes in solar radiation. The production and loss processes of ions and electrons have characteristic reaction times, resulting in a delayed response of electron density.

My early work investigated this response delay for individual solar flares and subsequently extended the analysis to a large sample of 455 solar flares spanning X-, M- and C-class events.

The analysis revealed dependencies of the response delay on flare class, season, latitude and altitude. In particular, the response delay was found to be generally higher for weaker C-class flares and lower for stronger X-class flares.

Altitude-Resolved D-Region Response

The initial studies of response time delay were performed at a fixed altitude. I subsequently extended the model to investigate the complete altitude dependence of both electron density and response time delay.

This altitude-resolved approach provides a more physically complete description of the D-region response and allows the variation of the response characteristics throughout the lower ionosphere to be investigated.

The resulting electron-density profiles later became particularly important for connecting the numerical ionospheric model with VLF radio propagation simulations.

D-Region Response Through Solar Cycle 24

My research was subsequently extended from individual solar flares to long-term solar variability.

For this purpose, the electron-continuity model was extended to incorporate both soft X-ray and EUV radiation, together with the dominant Lyman-α contribution during periods of low X-ray activity.

The model was used to investigate the D-region ionosphere throughout Solar Cycle 24 (2008–2019).

One of the notable results was the appearance of a dual-peak structure in the simulated ionization rate and electron-density variation, corresponding to the well-known dual-peak structure observed in the solar activity during the cycle.

The simulated electron-density profiles were also compared with the International Reference Ionosphere (IRI) model for validation.

From Electron Density to VLF Signals

Sub-ionospheric VLF propagation provides an established method for investigating the lower ionosphere. VLF signals propagate through the natural Earth–ionosphere waveguide and are sensitive to changes in D-region electron density.

A major objective of my later research was therefore to establish a direct connection between the electron-density model and observable VLF signal variations.

For this purpose, altitude-resolved electron-density profiles were converted into the ionospheric reflection parameters h′ and β using a log-linear extraction technique.

These parameters were then supplied to the Long Wave Propagation Capability (LWPC) code to calculate the corresponding VLF signal amplitude.

An Autonomous Model for VLF Signal Simulation

This led to the development of an integrated, largely autonomous modelling framework connecting solar radiation, D-region electron density and VLF signal propagation.

01

Electron Density

Solar X-ray/EUV inputs are used to solve the electron continuity equation and calculate D-region electron density.

02

Ionospheric Parameters

Altitude-resolved electron-density profiles are used to obtain Wait's empirical reflection parameters h′ and β.

03

VLF Simulation

The derived ionospheric parameters are supplied to LWPC to simulate the VLF signal amplitude.

The model can therefore operate from solar radiation input to simulated VLF observations without requiring an externally prescribed ionospheric reflection profile.

Multi-Path VLF Validation During Solar Flares

The integrated model was applied to VLF propagation during solar flares and validated against observed signal perturbations.

For long propagation paths, the transmitter–receiver great-circle path was divided into individual segments. The ionospheric electron density and the corresponding Wait parameters were calculated along the propagation path.

The resulting h′ and β profiles were then supplied to LWPC to simulate the VLF signal amplitude.

The simulated signals were compared with observed VLF measurements for multiple propagation paths, providing a direct validation of the complete modelling framework.

Quiet-Time VLF Propagation

The integrated approach was not restricted to solar flares. I also investigated the ability of the model to reproduce the normal diurnal behaviour of sub-ionospheric VLF signals during periods of relatively quiet solar activity.

A multi-station VLF dataset from the ICSP summer–winter campaigns of 2008–2009 was used for validation.

The model was used to calculate full-day VLF signal amplitudes using solar X-ray and EUV inputs. The simulated amplitudes showed good agreement with observations across different receiving stations and seasons.

This provides an important step towards using the electron-density-based approach for continuous simulation of VLF propagation under different ionospheric conditions.

Current and Future Directions

The modelling framework developed during my doctoral research provides a basis for extending the study towards broader solar–terrestrial and ionospheric problems.

  • Extending VLF signal modelling across an entire solar cycle to investigate long-term propagation variability.
  • Investigating the response of the D-region ionosphere to solar eclipse-induced changes in solar radiation using multi-station VLF observations.
  • Further developing the autonomous connection between solar observations, ionospheric modelling and radio-wave propagation.
  • Exploring the coupling between different regions of the ionosphere under changing solar and space-weather conditions.

The eclipse-related results will be added to this research portfolio as a separate research outcome.

Research Perspective

A recurring objective throughout my research has been to connect physical modelling with observable measurements. Rather than treating the ionosphere simply as a theoretical plasma, I aim to understand how changes in solar forcing produce measurable signatures in the Earth's near-space environment.

The combination of electron-continuity modelling and VLF remote sensing provides a particularly useful framework for this purpose. The model describes the physical evolution of the D-region, while VLF observations provide an independent experimental signature of that evolution.

From solar radiation to electron density, and from electron density to radio propagation — my research attempts to connect these stages into a single physical picture of solar–terrestrial coupling.
